About

24 July 2026 · Marc

I have a rather personal relationship with the web: somewhere between methodical curiosity and joyful entropy.

For me, a browsing session rarely follows a straight path. It starts with a question, veers off towards a tool, opens an article, three GitHub repositories and a video, then ends up with twelve thousand tabs spread across several windows, several browsers and sometimes several machines. This chaos isn’t just a bad habit: it’s also my way of exploring, connecting ideas and letting curiosity do its work.

The problem, of course, is finding what was really worth keeping.

A memory for my web#

I’ve been compiling Digests for years. They’re a mix of monitoring logs, annotated bookmarks and snapshots of my current obsessions: artificial intelligence, developer tools, design, photography, publishing, digital fabrication, free software and curiosities that are hard to categorise.

This site transforms this accumulation into a more enduring archive. The links are deduplicated, stripped of their tracking parameters as far as possible, categorised by subject and accompanied by a short summary. The archives allow you to find every edition, and the tags highlight the themes that recur over time.

The selection remains deliberately personal. It is neither an exhaustive news feed nor an automated collection of everything that comes along. I keep what intrigues me, what might prove useful, what fuels an idea — and sometimes things I don’t yet know what to do with.

Why ‘Digest’?#

The name is also a tribute to my grandad Richard. He was a subscriber to Reader’s Digest for almost his entire life. He used to make us read those books, talk to us about them and even give us the special children’s editions. Those volumes, which brought together stories, discoveries and windows onto the world, have always fascinated me.

Looking back, my Digest is, in a way, a continuation of that tradition: selecting, bringing together and passing on what has sparked curiosity. The medium has changed and the books have become links, but the desire remains the same — to share things that make you want to read, to understand and to explore further.

Images generated by code#

Each edition automatically produces a unique poster measuring 1,200 × 627 pixels. It is not created by AI: it is entirely generated by a graphics generator developed for the Digest.

A SHA-256 hash of the date, title and description initialises a deterministic pseudo-random generator. The same edition therefore always retains the same image, whilst each new Digest is given its own composition.

The system chooses from three graphic families — collisions, screens or broken grids — and then varies the structure, colours, rotations, patterns, ink textures, noise and visual anomalies. The colour scheme combines red, black and paper with an accent colour. The description, date and number of links are automatically incorporated, with line breaks and text length managed accordingly.

The image is first constructed in SVG. It is then rendered using Resvg, utilising exclusively the Bricolage Grotesque font provided by the site, without relying on any fonts installed on the server. The resulting PNG is finally optimised using Sharp: the colour palette is limited to 256 colours, compression is set to maximum, and the file size is kept below 500 KB.

Chance plays a part in the composition, but never in the reproducibility of the result.

Share on LinkedIn#

Sharing text, a URL and a large image simultaneously has proved unreliable with Open Graph previews and browser sharing tools.

The Digest therefore uses the LinkedIn API directly: the poster is uploaded as a media file, then linked to the text and URL of the edition. Before posting, a window allows you to draft the post, edit the hashtags generated from the Digest’s tags, and explicitly confirm publication.

This poster now has a square version measuring 1200 × 1200 pixels, distinct from the rectangular Open Graph image. It spans the full width of the feed. In the full-screen desktop viewer, LinkedIn may add two dark grey bands to centre the square within a taller column: these are part of the LinkedIn interface, not the image itself.

Uploading is synchronous, so that the post is only created once the image has actually been processed. A resource that has already been published remains reusable: the ‘Repost on LinkedIn’ action reopens the composer, allows you to change the text and hashtags, and then creates a new post without deleting the previous ones. The Digest thus retains a history of repeat posts whilst preventing concurrent submissions and unintended duplicates.

Geek, printer and photographer#

I’m Marc Tallec, a geek for long enough to have experienced several iterations of the Web, but also a printer and photographer. I enjoy understanding how a tool works just as much as seeing what it can be used to create: an image, a book, an interface, a service or a new way of working.

You can find my personal work at marctallec.com. Together with Claire, I’m also developing OOBLIK, our studio dedicated to imagery, print and editorial objects.

This Digest is therefore my space for reflection: an attempt to transform the noise, the open windows and my digital entropy into something shareable — the bits worth keeping… or not.
