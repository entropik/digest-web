Day 125 – The night of the two sessions

Day 125 of the diary: following the night of the Woo synchronisation and basket discounts, today’s work focuses on streamlining deletions and the checkout process, and preparing the dynamic product preview.

Quick read

Yesterday, the ‘Synchronise’ button was removed for product records. This night, the change has been extended to cover everything else: papers, workshop and library attributes, pricing matrices, MISKISS recipes, and the creation and deletion of products. The target is: a few seconds between a change in KEREDIT and its reflection on ooblik.com.

Meanwhile, another session is delivering basket discounts on WooCommerce — a feature that was anticipated, accepted, then reworked that very same night when the actual business rule (‘per visual’, not ‘per cumulative items’) replaced the first version. Two developers, a single repository, a single production environment: it’s going to be a long night.

The gap in coverage

The question that sets everything in motion is an innocent one: “If I rename a product in KEREDIT, do I need to do anything to see the change on WooCommerce?” Honest answer: no. The notification was only triggered when the product record was saved; shared repositories followed different paths. A paper renaming would only have been visible after the one-hour TTL had elapsed.

The chosen design: a catalogue-side plugin version. For targeted changes, we resynchronise the product; for changes to shared data sources, we invalidate everything and resynchronise in the background, triggered immediately by spawn_cron() without waiting for site traffic. On the backend, a three-second debounce prevents bursts of updates from triggering bursts of notifications.

The midnight collision

Plugin 0.1.90 is built, tested and ready for release. And then, the unthinkable: dev has changed during implementation. The ‘reset basket’ session has merged, bumping the application to 0.18.0 — and it has itself published a 0.1.90 plugin, without the synchronisation code. Same version number, two different sets of content. Publishing over the top would render any site that’s already up to date unusable: WordPress never offers the same version twice.

Decision made in thirty seconds: renumber. The catalogue synchronisation becomes 0.1.91, the changelog clearly distinguishes between the two batches, and the manifest is rebuilt with the actual checksum. The following night confirmed that this instinct was the right one: the version number of a published artefact is never reused.

The rebase, however, went badly the first time round — conflicts, stash, the scare of a ` reset --hard` on uncommitted user files, recovered just in time from a stash that had become dangling. A lesson learnt once again: when two sessions are working on the same worktree, stash early, stash often.

Deadlines don’t sleep

The deployment safeguard then rejects 0.18.1 until the version moves forward: unsurprisingly, 0.18.0 has just been deployed by the other session. Release branch, PR, CI, merge — the ritual, all over again. Then the 0.1.91 plugin is published and installed, the backend deployed, and the long-awaited test arrives at 2.03 am: renaming a document in KEREDIT. Four seconds later, _keredit_connector_synced_at is up and running on ooblik.com. The coverage is complete, the proof is in, the coffee’s cold.

The night isn’t over yet. Two minor issues are sorted out straight away: catalog-changed was returning a 400 instead of a 401 without credentials — WordPress validates route parameters before permission, a documented pitfall, and is locked down by a test — and the pricing matrices, such as the MISKISS are now included in the synchronisation scope. A new version numbering conflict arose in the process, as version 0.19.0 of the visual discounts was released ahead of the planned 0.18.2: the changelog entry was renumbered as 0.19.1, moved to the the top, and it is this version that goes into production that very night (the log is closed before the deployment is actually complete, subsequently verified: SHA served and container intact).

Opinion piece: are two agents better than one?

Night-time productivity is very real: three application versions, three plugin versions, ten merged PRs, two features delivered and tested in production. The hidden cost is just as real: version conflicts, rebases, contradictory manifests, and that particular kind of exhaustion that comes from having to re-read a changelog conflict at 3 am, wondering which session is correct.

The rule that emerges is not to slow down, but to make conflicts painless: version numbers that are never reused, small and frequent releases, deployment safeguards that reject rather than guess, and a single place — the changelog — to document afterwards who did what. The ‘Synchronise’ button, meanwhile, is permanently dormant.

The day after the night

The first post was written and merged at 3.11 am. It faithfully recounted the night, but not yet the day that was to follow. The recovery began with a very specific bug: deleting a discount slot only deleted its local draft. Upon reloading, the slot reappeared. The ‘Trash’ action has therefore become a truly persistent operation, with the draft restored in the event of failure and the card locked whilst the save is in progress. The convention is then documented: in a self-saving card, a destructive action cannot simply modify the React state.

The WooCommerce basket receives the same reliability treatment. Brief backend outages during redeployment should no longer alarm the customer with a permanent error. The plugin retries transient errors within a limited quota, retains an identical validation for two minutes, but always revalidates at checkout. The Codex review has tightened the quota and safeguards: an arbitrary integration header must not allow the limit to be circumvented, and wc-ajax=checkout must follow exactly the same contract.

The discount has reverted to its standard business rule

Last night’s functionality was still too complex. Following acceptance testing, the model has been simplified: a product family includes different products, regardless of the quantities printed. The volume discount on print runs remains part of the product price; the basket discount remains a family-level rule. This change removes more code than it adds and results in version 0.20.0.

At the same time, catalogue synchronisation has been made more robust: no notification when an import is rejected, a new run when a change occurs during resynchronisation, and more cautious finalisation of the pipeline. These changes will not be fully finalised until the following early morning, following the review feedback and the release of version 0.20.1.

The product is starting to take shape

The end of the day finally sees work begin on the visible aspect: separating the print-ready composition from the commercial presentation. A decision tree sets the course, then the implementation adds colours, textures and conditional designs that can be controlled via product options. These layers appear on the canvas and in the thumbnails, but remain out of thepages_datas and PDFs. This is a significant product milestone: KEREDIT can display a spiral, an elastic band or a cover without asking GOF to print them.

The day ends with an external incident: GitHub Actions needs to be restarted following an outage. The work is in place, but its merge and release are scheduled for 7 August. The initial commit was therefore just in time for the night; this catch-up gives it the second session it was missing.
