Solar eclipse seen through a Swiss grid

An eclipse becomes the starting point for an identity made up of Bricolage Grotesque, JetBrains Mono, black, paper and coral. The Journal du Digest takes shape with Codex through a series of prompts, trials and checks within the browser. This process opens up a second narrative: preserving and sharing URLs, even when the web returns a 404 error.

The eclipse as a method

On 12 August, the sun disappears just enough to remind us that one form can become more legible when another recedes. This is a good definition of the design of the Digest: not adding decoration, but organising the absences, the intervals, the black spaces and what must remain visible.

The journal therefore does not seek to resemble a blog. It borrows from Swiss design its explicit grid, its firm alignments and its unambiguous hierarchy. It borrows from the architect’s title block the adjoining cells, the shared lines, the date, the folio number and the sense that a page belongs to a larger whole. The result builds on the language developed in *From the Swiss Cartouche to the Memory of the OOBLIK Blog*, but applies it here to the construction phase rather than to categories.

Black provides the structure. Off-white retains the warmth of the paper. Grey carries the metadata without obscuring it. Coral serves as a signal: a number, an active marker, a hover link, a small burst of light in an almost monochrome composition. As during an eclipse, the colour derives its power solely from its scarcity.

Two typographic voices

Bricolage Grotesque carries the headings. Its width, its irregularities and its energy prevent the grid from becoming bureaucratic. It can be monumental in a post, dense on a map, and retain a very physical presence when the heading spans several lines.

JetBrains Mono handles everything else: dates, summaries, captions, links, technical snippets. This monospace typeface bears the mark of the tool and the source file. It conveys that the journal is not only written, but also constructed. The duo avoids two pitfalls: a Swiss modernity that is too museum-like and a development interface that is too literal.

American posters from the 1940s and 1950s add a third voice. They do not serve as nostalgic wallpaper: their solid colours, diagonals and graphic effectiveness respond to the grid. In the index, they form cropped banners. In the article, they revert to square objects, attributed to and linked with their public collection.

To explore in the Digest: design, typography and graphic design.

Building in dialogue with Codex

The page did not emerge from a perfect specification. It took shape as we watched it come to life. I describe an intention — ‘architect’s cartouche’, ‘more dense’, ‘the title must breathe’, ‘no bullet points’ — and Codex translates this direction into Hugo templates, CSS, responsive rules and tests.

This division of labour is important. I retain control over the direction, the aesthetic, the memory of the project and the right to say when something feels off. I leave the technical side to Codex: finding the right file, understanding the content adapter, preserving the nine existing posts, setting up the RSS feed, checking the previous and next links, preventing mobile overflow, and then displaying the result in a real browser.

The prompt isn’t a magic command. It’s a design tool. An initial formulation provides a structure. A screenshot allows us to highlight a detail. Hovering reveals a colour that’s too muted. The mobile view shows excessive density. We make corrections, reload, compare. Testing then takes over to transform a visual preference into a lasting rule.

This approach ties in with Administration and curation via Chrome: the tool is not separate from its use. The browser is for viewing, Codex for building and verifying, and Git for keeping a record. The technology becomes accessible without taking centre stage.

A journal made up of links

The Digest was born out of a somewhat unreasonable passion for URLs. An address is tiny, but it holds a promise: someone has published something somewhere, and accepts that we can access it without asking permission from a platform.

Collecting a link isn’t just about setting it aside. It’s about giving it a fitting title, a few words of context, tags, a date and a place amongst other discoveries. It’s about creating a way back for yourself, and then a way in for others. Archives, the Internet, the Web’s memory and the Web itself are not static shelves: they enable connections that search engines and algorithmic feeds no longer offer.

Internal links matter just as much as external ones. They connect the different stages of the project: the creation of the Digest and the restoration of lost links, the revival of tags, and finally the legacy of the OOBLIK Blog. The journal thus forms neither a closed chronology nor a cold record, but a map in which each post can reopen several paths.

Learning to love 404s

A 404 error does not erase what once existed. It merely changes its status. Where an ordinary catalogue would delete an address that has become obsolete, the Digest retains it, marks it as dead and documents what it once allowed us to see. Absence becomes information.

This attention is not a cult of ruins. It champions a simple idea of the open internet: pages published at their own addresses, freely linked, copied, cited and shared without restriction. Domains change hands, services close down, names move on. Preserving the original URL ensures that history is not rewritten after the event.

The launch of the Digest already established this principle by bringing together scattered HTML Digests, blogs and social feeds. The journal now gives it a visible form. The nets are the connections, the cells are the fragments of memory, and the coral marks what remains active amidst the disappearances.

Long-term decisions

  • keep ‘Bricolage Grotesque’ for titles and ‘JetBrains Mono’ for the text.
  • Use the coral as a subtle highlight, particularly when hovering over headings.
  • Design the navigation like an architect’s title block, never as a collection of rounded buttons.
  • Build with Codex through visible iterations, then convert the choices into reproducible tests.
  • Increase the number of internal links between posts, tags and archives.
  • Keep dead URLs for reference without hiding their status.
  • Reserve LinkedIn posting for explicit action by the administration.

Note on provenance

This post is a retrospective design note, based on the visual and editorial choices made during the project. It does not claim to reproduce word for word a recorded conversation, nor to attribute to 12 August any commits made after that date.

Continuation

Continue to write the journal as one might tend a constellation: adding points, linking days, noting extinctions and leaving enough space for a new path to emerge.
