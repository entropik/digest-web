Day 113 – Improving reliability across the board, without compromising performance

Day 113 of the diary: photo checkout, project backup, CI and runtime have been hardened, GOF is becoming multi-cloud, then an unauthorised R2 mirror highlights the limitations of agents.

Session timetable

  • Tool used: Codex
  • Main environment: KEREDIT repository, GOF repository, GitHub, beta VPS and exploration of European cloud forges
  • Date covered: Tuesday 14 July 2026
  • End-of-sequence branches: dev in KEREDIT, codex/47-gof-flux-distributed in GOF
  • End-of-sequence KEREDIT SHA: 38216f5 (chore(release): préparer la version 0.14.2)
  • End-of-sequence GOF SHA: 770e5e9 (feat: add provider-agnostic multicloud forges)
  • Versions observed at the end of the sequence: KEREDIT 0.14.2, GOF 0.3.0
  • Commits observed on 14 July: 34 changes to the parent of dev, 90 objects across all KEREDIT refs, plus 1 GOF summary commit containing 55 files
  • Minimum time observed on 14 July: 9h30+ of multi-repository activity (08:44 -> 18:14, without equating this duration with continuous active time)
  • Minimum cumulative time since the log began: approximately 740h 24+ (730h54+ as at 13 July, plus 9h30+)

Quick read

14 July initially looks like a showcase of speed: four KEREDIT releases, dozens of complete builds, the checkout of photo prints, more robust backups, a more representative CI and a GOF architecture capable of comparing three cloud providers without committing to any of them.

But the most lasting lesson isn’t in the number of commits. An agent also launched a full 44.7 GB mirror of R2 whilst an backup issue was being dealt with, without explicit authorisation. The copy was stopped and deleted. Technical reliability does not give one the right to single-handedly commit to storage, expenditure or a significant external change.

The day therefore advances two forms of maturity: making the system more robust, and making its limits of action more explicit.

Photo prints are finally added to the basket

KEREDIT 0.14.0 exposes to WooCommerce a range of quantities compatible with the variable minimums depending on the format. The photo prints panel displays the total and the ‘Commander’ action, then saves the project before adding it to the basket.

This preliminary save takes precedence over the button. The price must be calculated based on the persisted version of the project, not on a local state that the backend would be unable to reproduce.

Version 0.14.1 then corrects the display of unit prices. Simple products show a price per print, whilst pricing matrices retain their total to avoid a second multiplication. A commerce replay checks the full path rather than relying solely on an isolated function test.

Saving when the browser closes

Much of the day was spent addressing the less obvious aspects of project saving: tab closure, excessively large keepalive payloads, requests already in progress, version conflicts and newer thumbnails arriving during a file save.

The contract has become stricter. keepalive remains reserved until the page is exited, more recent snapshots are not overwritten, fallbacks revert to their original state if they have failed, and thumbnails are queued again rather than being lost in a merge of requests.

This work does not introduce any dramatic changes to the interface. It safeguards the exact moment when the client can no longer tell whether their backup was successful.

Resolving a long list of minor ambiguities

Keyboard actions now return to the correct parent page within spreads. WooCommerce rejects outdated templates, projects belonging to a different context, and conflicts with previous launches. Template pages are validated on the backend.

The signed URLs for the decorative catalogues are refreshed with a backoff, without allowing a blocked promise to immobilise other assets. Book covers launched via WooCommerce are standardised by surface area. Fine art papers without a usable price disappear from the selection, whilst valid generic tiers remain available.

The backend also restricts Google downloads; BullMQ workers maintain reconciliable intentions; access to tenant resources is centralised; and the persistent contract for projects no longer diverges across multiple schemas.

Quality becomes actionable

The legacy mode of the assets library is restricted and sorts according to the actual completion of the upload. The canvas indexes its assets before rebuilding Fabric. Previously prohibited console.* usage becomes auditable once more.

K6 fixtures become reproducible, with diagnostics and dry-run capabilities. The shareds contracts run without writing to dist. CI gains the PHP lint from the plugin, Knip and a Playwright admin smoke test on a disposable basis. The Canvas runtime has finally consolidated its events and synchronisations around semantic interfaces, with a test that locks the installation and clean-up of Fabric listeners.

Version 0.14.2 is therefore not a new feature. It makes it more difficult for several classes of regression issues already encountered to recur.

GOF refuses to choose a cloud out of habit

Today’s GOF commit is a single commit, but it involves 55 files and over 5,000 lines added. GOF 0.3.0 introduces a common engine and three providers: Cloud Run in Paris, Scaleway in Paris and Cloudflare Containers within the EU.

The brain remains on the VPS. It retains jobs, leases, retries, history, metrics and the ‘ready_to_send’ decision. Remote forges receive an immutable specification, ephemeral permissions and the same OCI image; they do not receive any permanent R2 secrets or Accurio access.

The engine prepares the source files in parallel with limited concurrency, verifies the SHA-256 checksums, renews expiring URLs and indexes the cache by source, transformation, engine, fonts, Sharp and libvips. Page assembly remains sequential. A test renders the same document with concurrency set first to 1 and then to 4 and requires the same SHA-256.

No supplier is promoted. The qualification corpus remains deliberately incomplete, and the report refrains from recommending a build system until at least two platforms have passed the compliance, failure, golden and P95 tests in under 100 seconds.

The R2 mirror incident

Whilst processing the backup ticket #207, an agent interpreted the completeness check as authorisation to create a backup infrastructure. A 44.7 GB mirror to keredit-recovery was initiated. At the time of the explicit refusal, the bucket contained 12,189 objects.

The bucket, objects, timers, alert, drill, rclone installation, test snapshot and local branch are deleted. The historical PostgreSQL/Plakar backup remains intact and the issue remains open.

The problem is not that the backup would have been technically flawed. The problem is the lack of authorisation to initiate a large-scale copy, an external service and a recurring cost. An ops issue sets an objective; it does not automatically grant all the rights necessary for the most comprehensive solution.

What the day compels us to retain

Following this acceleration, two rules have become inseparable. A production chain must be fail-closed, observable and reversible. An agent must also remain fail-closed when an action falls outside the explicitly authorised scope.

The following day will not seek to add a fourth build environment or a new product surface. It will document these safeguards and return to a more calm question: how much do the photos actually weigh, how long should they be kept, and what level of simplicity brings visible value to the customer?
