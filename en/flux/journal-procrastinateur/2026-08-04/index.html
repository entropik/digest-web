Day 123 – Measuring the load, keeping the house

Day 123 of the log: the publisher campaign expands to 50 virtual users; security and CI audits are brought up to scratch; then the log catches up on the backlog caused by a busy period and holidays.

Quick read

The day begins with measurement and ends with tidying up. KEREDIT now has a more realistic load campaign, the transitive vulnerabilities in the CI gate have been resolved, and the security audit has become a scheduled task on the repository runner.

Then the work turns to its own history. The log had not progressed since 22 July. Time, the holidays and fatigue had led one to believe there was a massive backlog; the records reveal a gentler reality: nine calendar days without a commit, a few very busy days, and above all, posts that could still be reconstructed without having to make things up.

A release that feels more like a beta

PR #369 reworks the editor session scenario around the current login, explicit thresholds and a selective payload manifest. The campaign reaches 50 virtual users with a reproducible report, without loading all image sets in every scenario.

This work does not guarantee that the beta will handle every peak. It provides a replayable baseline, observable thresholds and a common vocabulary for comparing the next campaign with this one.

Security must be addressed every week

PR #370 aligns fast-uri and brace-expansion with the versions addressing the security advisories tracked by the repository. PR #371 adds a weekly audit and standardises the use of the Mac runner keredit-ci as a known service on the workstation.

The key change is the frequency. A one-off audit indicates that the repository was clean on a given day; a scheduled audit allows us to see when it ceases to be so.

Cleaning up without erasing history

Local tidying removes old GOF checkouts, archives a NAS context file and sends unwanted artefacts to the Bin. Version history retains the list, the justifications and the items deliberately kept.

The same principle applies to the log: we can remove noise without deleting the history. The entry from 24 June is restored, the one from 22 July is complete, and those from 23 July, 30 July and 3 August are returned to their correct places.

Time is recalculated

The previous cumulative total of 740h19+ was too low. It omitted 24 minutes on 1 May, six hours on 24 June, and four days from 25 to 28 June, which were left out of the calculation. The update adds 41h05+ even before the end of July, then reinstates the long evening of 22 July, the sessions on 23 and 30 July and the one on 3 August.

This total remains a minimum. It does not convert breaks into working time and does not claim to measure CI expectations, conversations, manual tests or fatigue exactly. It simply brings the tally back into line with what the records still allow us to prove.
