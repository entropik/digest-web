Day 111 – When the forge becomes an economic decision

Day 111 of the log: source KEREDIT, two valid forges and Accurio, measurement cache and Cloud Run driver compared with Company X’s costs.

Quick read

The day is no longer simply about keeping two forges running. It proves that their work remains traceable, recoverable and measurable when an agent disappears or an attempt needs to be replayed.

This technical discipline raises another question: how much does autonomy really cost? An internal comparison with Company X shows that the forge is not merely a production site. It is also an economic choice.

KEREDIT finalises the provenance contract

The first benchmark reveals a useful anomaly: the 24 checksums declared by the golden payload no longer correspond to the print-ready objects actually read by GOF. Silent normalisation would have produced a quick but false test result.

KEREDIT therefore adds a checksum specific to the print-ready derivative and a backfill that re-reads the storage. The migration successfully verifies 272 out of 272 checksums.

A new export of the ‘#1075781’ command arrives in GOF with 24 sources out of 24 verifiable.

The fix is delivered in KEREDIT 0.12.1. A second batch also strengthens the deployment process to push the verified HEAD to the VPS. Provenance is no longer a promise between the two repositories: it becomes a pre-release verification contract.

The final safeguard finally prevents a ‘derive print’ from being exported to GOF if it is not actually ready. The presence of a URL is no longer sufficient to classify a source as production.

GOF tightens up and then accepts distributed forges

The pilot first restores cockpit navigation and stabilises the PDF preview during surveys. The work then moves down into the control plane: leases, attempts, resumption, clean-up and source identification.

An abandoned attempt must no longer lose the forge’s useful state. A resumption must not mix two executions. Distributed deployment gains safeguards before affecting multiple machines.

Standardised storage gains PostgreSQL fencing, an export and a restore of its complete state. Two replicas must not be able to validate the same transaction as if they were alone.

The live acceptance test goes further: the P340 and the i5 each render the cover and the KEREDIT ‘golden’ specification using the same font release. Thirty consecutive jobs complete ‘rendered’ without failure or stalling.

Following a joint restart of the control plan and the dashboard, the thirty reports are retrieved from PostgreSQL and both agents return to the ‘ready’ status. The historical GOF remains available throughout the acceptance test.

The acceptance testing proceeds to a real Accurio submission, which is explicitly authorised. The ‘KEREDIT00067’ order, item ‘KEREDIT00067-1’, returns with ‘ isSubmissionValid=true’, after which the two live locks are released.

The P340 migration output and the Accurio test are accepted. The monolith is not yet removed: the final hurdle remains the switch from the control plan to the standardised tables.

Measure the weight before promising speed

Optimisation moves beyond intuition. GOF retains the prepared resources, limits transformations, builds seeds offline and flags source checksums that have become obsolete.

Golden samples provide quality assurance against time measurements. The aim is not to save a few seconds by sacrificing a font, native text or PDF compliance.

On the golden sample comprising 28 source pages and 24 assets, the P340 achieves a cold P50 of 63.140 seconds and a warm P50 of 5.633 seconds. The i5 achieves 77.589 seconds when cold and 6.980 seconds when warm.

The P340 therefore comes close to the sub-minute target without quite achieving it when cold. The pre-render cache, however, removes 100 per cent of the Sharp processing on the hot run and brings both machines below 7.1 seconds, including the qpdf inspection.

Forty qpdf inspections are completed. The 25 rasterised pages are identical between the two systems, as are the boxes, fonts and validation codes. The conservative setting 1 x Sharp 1 becomes the benchmark.

This baseline changes the issue of parallelism. Increasing the pool only makes sense if a batch benchmark improves throughput without increasing memory usage or causing quality metrics to diverge.

Cloud Run becomes an executable POC

The same core gains a Cloud Run runner, profiles 4-16, 6-24 and 8-32, atomic attempts, start-up measurement and a release of 1,341 fonts verified within the image.

The control plan remains at flux-test.keredit.com. The Cloud container takes a lease, returns it, validates and publishes, but receives neither general R2 secrets nor Accurio access. The P340 and i5 remain the permanent local backups.

Cloud Run is not yet the nominal build environment. The three profiles must still pass five runs, the failure tests and a P95 strictly under 100 seconds before any automatic prioritisation.

Company X’s 2025 archives provide a benchmark: 3,598 PDFs occupy approximately 183 GB, or 50.9 MB per file. The observed average GOF in staging is around 32.5 MB per PDF.

At a constant volume, the GOF projection falls to 117 GB. This is not yet an annual guarantee, but the direction of the saving is consistent with the pipeline: images reduced to their print-ready format, JPEGs for quality control and text retained in its native format.

What Company X’s invoice reveals

Company X charges approximately 900 euros for server costs, 4,788 euros for subscription fees and 8,000 euros in commissions. The total comes to around 13,688 euros per year.

KEREDIT’s two Hetzner servers do not cost 900 euros per year. The May invoice is for 44.88 euros excluding VAT, which amounts to approximately 646 euros including VAT on an annualised basis. The conservative budget rounds this up to 650 euros per year.

Cloud Run, R2 and ancillary services bring the conservative GOF scenario to around 687 euros per year. The difference then amounts to approximately 13,001 euros, or almost 95 per cent of Company X’s current cost.

When scaled back to the 2,389 orders in 2025, the operating cost falls from around €5.73 to €0.29 per order.

Even in a ‘hard’ scenario, with two new Hetzner servers at 2026 public rates and a high cloud margin, the cost remains close to 2,129 euros per year. The saving therefore remains in excess of 11,500 euros.

A significant saving, but not without cost

This calculation does not account for development, migration or manual maintenance. Nor does it yet quantify the HD originals, proxies and reports that may accumulate in R2.

It highlights something else: the subscription and commissions do not merely fund hardware. They fund a dependency. Replacing this dependency shifts the cost towards expertise, control and maintenance.

Part of the expenditure will therefore need to remain within the system: backups, security, observability, golden tests and human time. Autonomy without a maintenance budget would simply be a new debt.

What remains to be measured before the switchover

The next step is to run the full Cloud Run benchmark, then track its cost per job, the weight per page and per order, the median, the P95, the retries and failures by product family.

We also need to measure the actual R2 volume, separate inputs and outputs, then set a retention period. The 183 GB remains Company X’s historical figure. The 117 GB remains a GOF projection to be confirmed in production.

The day ends with four further pieces of evidence: the source contract is finalised, two production environments have passed acceptance testing, Accurio has accepted a live order and the Cloud POC is now ready for deployment.

If the economic assumptions are confirmed, GOF will give KEREDIT considerable manoeuvring room without sacrificing its offline insurance cover.
